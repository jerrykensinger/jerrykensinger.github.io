Mobile connectivity

From an expensive wireless bill to a two-network strategy.

What started as a simple effort to lower a Verizon Wireless bill turned into a much more interesting experiment: how to spend less on cellular service without giving up the ability to get connected when one network struggles.

The problem: paying too much for the default setup

Our Verizon Wireless bill had grown to the point where I no longer felt the monthly cost made sense. Instead of treating the bill as something fixed, I started breaking it apart: what were we actually paying for, which features did we really use, and how much of the premium was simply the cost of staying with the familiar carrier?

01
Reset the monthly cost

I moved our primary service away from the traditional postpaid model and into Visible. Because Visible uses Verizon's network, it gave me a way to keep the coverage footprint I was accustomed to while dramatically reducing the recurring service cost.

02
Pay attention to real-world performance

The next lesson was that a phone can show a strong signal and still have a poor data experience. Congestion, building construction, network priority, and the specific location can all matter. A network that works great at home may not be the best network at a school, arena, workplace, or while traveling.

03
Add a second network instead of replacing the first

That led me to dual SIM. Rather than trying to find one carrier that is perfect everywhere, a modern iPhone can keep a primary line and a second eSIM available at the same time. The second line can be on a different carrier—or even a pay-as-you-go data provider—so there is another path when the primary network is congested or weak.

04
Use the second SIM only when it earns its keep

My preferred approach is a low-cost primary service for everyday use and a second network that can be activated for data when needed. That can mean manually switching cellular data at a difficult location, keeping a backup eSIM ready for travel, or using a second number when it is useful.

The takeaway

I stopped thinking of a cell phone plan as one carrier I had to live with everywhere. Now I think of the phone as a device that can choose between networks.

Dual SIM does not combine two weak signals into one stronger signal. Its value is redundancy: if one network is poor because of geography, a building, congestion, or local coverage, the other network may perform much better.
